Module 3

Difficult Conversations

Every gym school teaches you how to coach. None of them teach you what to say when a long-term member wants to cancel, or how to announce a price increase without apologizing five times. This module is those conversations.

Why this is the hardest part of running a gym

The operational side of a gym — programming, scheduling, invoicing — has software. The human side does not. When a member says they are canceling, no system tells you what to say. When someone leaves a 2-star review, no app writes your response. When you need to tell a member their behavior is not acceptable, you are on your own.

Most gym owners either avoid these conversations, handle them in a way they later regret, or spend an hour drafting an email that still feels wrong when they send it. The anxiety is real and the cost is real — in lost revenue, damaged relationships, and staff situations that fester instead of resolve.

Claude does not replace your judgment. But it does give you a draft, a structure, and a tone to react to — which is almost always faster and better than starting from a blank page.

Before you start

Each prompt in this module has a line that says [PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]. That refers to the Gym Profile Block you built in Module 1. Paste it there every time. Without it, Claude gives you a generic response. With it, every output sounds like it came from your gym.

Conversation 1 — The Cancellation Save

A member reaches out to cancel. Your first instinct is to feel defensive or to let them go without a word. Neither is right. This prompt helps you prepare for the call or conversation — what to say, how to listen, and what to offer that might actually address their real reason.

When to use this: Anytime a member notifies you they want to cancel — by text, email, or in person. Use it before the conversation, not after.

I need help preparing for a member cancellation conversation.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Member situation:
- Member's first name: [NAME]
- How long they have been a member: [DURATION]
- Reason they gave for canceling: [REASON — e.g., "too expensive," "schedule changed," "not using it enough," "moving"]
- Their original goal when they joined (if known): [GOAL, or "not recorded"]
- Anything else relevant: [DETAILS, or "nothing specific"]

Give me three things:

1. A brief honest read on whether this cancellation sounds recoverable and why — one short paragraph, no sugarcoating.

2. A conversational script for a phone call or face-to-face conversation. Not robotic — the way a real person talks. Include what to say at the start, how to ask about their real reason, and one specific offer or alternative that addresses what they told you. Do not guilt-trip them or use high-pressure sales language.

3. A short follow-up message I can send after the conversation regardless of outcome — text or email, under 100 words — that leaves the door open without being pushy.

The goal is to understand their reason first, then respond to that. If they are firm, help me end on good terms.
Example Output — Cancellation Situation

Situation: Marcus, 14 months as a member, says he's canceling because "it's getting expensive." Original goal: build strength, lose weight. Still making progress.

Claude's read: This sounds like a price-sensitivity cancellation that may be recoverable — not a disengaged member. Marcus has been here 14 months and was making progress, which means the value is real; the question is whether the payment feels proportionate to his life right now. Worth a conversation.

Script: "Hey Marcus, thanks for reaching out. Before I process anything I just wanted to give you a quick call — is now an okay time? ... I hear you on the cost — that's totally fair and I'm not going to try to talk you out of anything. Can I ask, is it the monthly amount itself, or is it more that the timing is rough right now? ... [if timing] We can actually pause your membership for up to 60 days — no charge, your rate is locked when you come back. Would that help?" ...

Follow-up message: "Marcus — good talking with you. Whatever you decide, you're always welcome back here. The progress you made in 14 months was real. — Coach John"

Conversation 2 — Price Increase Announcement

Most gym owners either delay price increases for years because they dread the announcement, or they write an email that apologizes so many times it undermines the decision entirely. This prompt produces a direct, confident announcement that respects your members enough to be honest with them.

When to use this: Any time you are raising rates — annual increases, post-renovation, post-staffing expansion, or your first increase in years.

Help me write a price increase announcement for my gym members.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Details:
- Current rate (main membership): [CURRENT PRICE per month]
- New rate: [NEW PRICE per month]
- When the new rate takes effect: [DATE]
- How much notice members are getting: [WEEKS/MONTHS]
- Honest reason for the increase: [REASON — e.g., "rent went up," "we added a second coach," "first increase in three years and costs have risen," "new equipment investment"]
- Any exceptions: [DETAILS — e.g., "members on annual plans are locked until renewal," or "no exceptions"]
- How members can reach out with questions: [EMAIL or "reply to this message"]

Write one email announcement that:
- Opens with the news directly — no long preamble
- States the reason honestly in one short paragraph, without being defensive
- Acknowledges that price increases are never welcome
- Reminds members what they are getting and why it is worth it — in one confident paragraph, not a list of bullet points
- States clearly what happens next and when
- Closes with confidence, not an apology

Do not use: "we hate to do this," "we know this is hard news," "we truly appreciate your understanding," or any phrase that reads like the gym is asking for forgiveness. Under 300 words total.
Example Output — Price Increase Email

Subject: A change to your membership rate starting July 1

Starting July 1st, our monthly membership rate is increasing from $150 to $165.

Here is why: our lease renewed at a higher rate, and we made the decision to absorb it for eight months before passing any of it on. We could not do that indefinitely. This is the first rate increase at Iron Standard in three years.

What does not change: the coaching, the programming, the equipment, or the standard of what you get when you walk through the door. That is what you are paying for, and we take it seriously.

If you have an annual membership, your rate is locked until your renewal date. Month-to-month members will see the new rate on their July invoice. If you have questions, reply to this email and I will respond personally.

— Coach John, Iron Standard

Conversation 3 — Member Code-of-Conduct Warning

A member has done something that needs to be addressed — a comment that made another member uncomfortable, repeated tardiness that disrupts class, or something more serious. You need to say something, in writing, that is clear and professional enough to stand as documentation if it happens again.

When to use this: Any time you need to address a member's behavior formally — whether in person, by message, or both.

I need to address a behavior issue with a gym member.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Situation:
- Member's first name: [NAME]
- What happened: [DESCRIBE SPECIFICALLY — what they did, when, and who was affected]
- Has this happened before with this member: [YES / NO — if yes, briefly describe]
- What was said verbally already (if anything): [DETAILS, or "nothing yet"]
- What specifically needs to change: [THE BEHAVIOR]
- Consequence if it continues: [e.g., "membership will be suspended," "membership will be terminated"]

Give me two things:

1. A written message — email or text — that states what happened factually, what needs to change, and what happens if it does not. No loaded language. No anger. No excessive apology either. Professional, calm, and specific. Written so it could serve as documentation if needed.

2. A short version I could say out loud if I address this in person first — 3 to 5 sentences, the way a real person talks.

The goal is to be clear and give this person a genuine chance to correct the behavior — not to back them into a corner.
Example Output — Written Warning

Written message:

Hi Jordan — I want to follow up on what happened during Tuesday's 6am class. A comment you made to another member during the workout was reported to me as making them uncomfortable. I am not going to detail it here, but I want you to know I take this seriously.

Iron Standard is a gym where every member should feel respected. That standard applies to everyone, including in off-hand comments between sets. I am asking you to be more mindful of how you interact with other members going forward.

If something like this comes to my attention again, I will need to suspend or terminate your membership. I do not want to do that — you have been a member here for two years and I value having you here. But I also cannot let it slide.

If you want to talk about it, I am happy to do that. — Coach John

In-person version: "Jordan, I need to talk with you about something that came up after Tuesday. A comment you made was reported to me, and I want to address it directly. Our standard here is that every member feels respected — and I need you to hold that standard. If it happens again, I'll need to make some decisions about your membership. I'm not trying to make this a big deal, but I did need to say something."

Conversation 4 — Negative Review Response

A bad review lands on Google or Yelp. Your first instinct — to either ignore it or write a defensive paragraph — is almost always wrong. The response you write is not for the reviewer. It is for every future member reading it. This prompt helps you write something that de-escalates, sounds human, and moves the conversation offline.

When to use this: Within 24–48 hours of a negative review appearing. Speed matters — future readers notice how long it takes to respond.

Help me respond to a negative review about my gym.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

The review:
- Platform: [Google / Yelp / Facebook]
- What the review says: [PASTE THE REVIEW TEXT]
- Is the complaint accurate: [YES / PARTIALLY / NO — explain briefly]
- Context the public cannot see: [DETAILS, or "nothing to add"]

Write a public response that:
- Acknowledges the reviewer's experience without being defensive or dismissive
- Is under 100 words — short responses look more confident than long ones
- Does not get into specifics that could escalate the conversation publicly
- Invites them to contact you directly with a specific method (email or phone)
- Sounds like a real person wrote it, not a PR template

If the complaint is factually inaccurate, correct it in one sentence — calmly and specifically, not aggressively. Then move to the invitation.

Do not use: "we are sorry you feel that way," "we take all feedback seriously," "we pride ourselves on," or any phrase that sounds automated.
Example Output — Review Response

The review: "Showed up for my first class and nobody explained anything to me. Just thrown in with everyone else. Not what I expected for the price."

Response:

Hi — that is not the experience we want anyone to have on their first day, and I am sorry it landed that way. New members should be introduced to the class format and checked in with before things start — that clearly did not happen here. I would genuinely like to talk with you about it and make it right. You can reach me directly at john@ironstandard.com. — Coach John

Conversation 5 — Staff Performance Feedback

A coach is consistently late. A front desk person is not following up with leads. You need to say something, but you are not an HR department and you do not want to make it worse. This prompt helps you prepare for the conversation and document what was agreed.

When to use this: Before a performance conversation with a coach or staff member. Do this prep work, then have the conversation in person.

Help me prepare for a performance conversation with a staff member.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Situation:
- Role: [COACH / FRONT DESK / MANAGER]
- How long they have worked here: [DURATION]
- The issue: [DESCRIBE SPECIFICALLY — e.g., "arriving 10 minutes late to 6am classes three times in the last month," "not following up with new inquiry leads within 24 hours," "members have mentioned they feel dismissed during sessions"]
- Has this come up before: [YES / NO]
- What a good outcome looks like: [WHAT NEEDS TO CHANGE]
- Your relationship with this person: [GOOD / NEUTRAL / STRAINED]

Give me two things:

1. Talking points for the in-person conversation — structured as: what I have observed, what the impact has been, what I need to change, and what support I can offer. Direct and honest without being harsh. Short enough that I can actually use it as notes.

2. A written follow-up I can send the same day that summarizes what was discussed and what was agreed to. This serves as a record without feeling like a formal warning unless the situation warrants it.

The goal is to give this person a clear, fair chance to improve — not to build a case for firing them.
Example Output — Staff Feedback Prep

Talking points:

What I've observed: "You've been late to the 6am class three times in the last month — by 8–12 minutes each time. I've noted the dates." · Impact: "When the class starts without you, members either wait around or just start on their own. It sets a tone, and a few have mentioned it." · What needs to change: "6am means you're there by 5:50, warmed up and ready. That's the standard for everyone." · Support: "If the shift is genuinely hard to make, I'd rather know that now and figure something out than have this become a recurring issue."

Written follow-up:

"Hey — thanks for talking with me today. To confirm what we discussed: the expectation is that you're on the floor and ready 10 minutes before every class, including 6am. We agreed this is the standard going forward. I'll check in with you in two weeks to see how it's going. — John"

One rule for all of these

Claude drafts. You decide. Read what it produces, adjust anything that does not sound like you, and then use it. The goal is never to send something verbatim without reading it. The goal is to spend five minutes getting to a first draft instead of forty-five.

The more specific your inputs — especially the member's name, the exact situation, and your Gym Profile — the more specific and usable the output. Vague inputs produce generic outputs. Be specific and you will rarely need to rewrite more than a sentence or two.
