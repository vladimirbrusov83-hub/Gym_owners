Hiring & Staff

Small gyms hire rarely and get it wrong often. The job posting attracts the wrong people. The interview is a conversation that goes nowhere. The new coach is confused for their first month. Claude can handle all of it — start to finish.

Why hiring is hard for gym owners

You are not a hiring manager. You have never run an HR department and you should not have to. But when you need a coach or a front desk person, the whole process falls on you — writing the post, screening the applications, running the interview, and getting them up to speed fast enough that they are actually useful.

Most gym owners write a job posting that lists requirements instead of describing the job. They conduct interviews that feel like conversations without a purpose. They onboard new staff by showing them around for an hour and hoping for the best. Then they wonder why the hire did not work out.

The prompts in this module walk you through the full hiring cycle — from the first job post to the end of the first week. Use the ones you need, skip the ones you do not.

Tool 1 — Job Posting

A good gym job posting does not lead with a list of requirements. It leads with who you are and what the job actually is — so the right people apply and the wrong ones self-select out.

Write a job posting for an open position at my gym.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Role details:
- Position title: [e.g., "Part-Time Coach," "Front Desk / Member Experience," "Head Strength Coach"]
- Hours per week: [RANGE]
- Pay: [HOURLY RATE or SALARY RANGE, or "competitive — discussed at interview"]
- Required certifications or qualifications: [LIST, or "none required — we will train the right person"]
- What this person does day-to-day: [3–5 bullet points — be specific and honest]
- What matters most to you in this hire beyond the resume: [e.g., "reliability, genuine curiosity about members' progress, someone who takes initiative without being asked"]
- What makes working here different from a commercial gym: [1–2 honest sentences]
- How to apply: [EMAIL, DM, form, etc.]

Write a job posting that:
- Opens with who we are, not a list of requirements
- Describes the role in plain language — what they will actually do each day
- States clearly what kind of person fits here, not just what credentials they need
- Includes one honest sentence about what this job is not — to filter wrong-fit applicants before they apply
- Ends with clear application instructions
- Is under 350 words and sounds like a person wrote it
Example Output — Part-Time Coach Posting

Part-Time Strength Coach — Iron Standard, St. Louis

Iron Standard is a small, strength-focused gym built around barbell training. We have about 80 members, one head coach, and a front desk person. Most of our members are working adults who train 3–4 times a week and care more about getting stronger over time than about finding the hardest workout possible.

We are looking for a part-time coach for early morning and weekend shifts — roughly 15–18 hours per week, with room to grow. Pay is $22–26/hour depending on experience.

Day to day, you will run small group classes, write programs for a small personal training roster, and generally be someone members look forward to seeing. You will also set up the gym before opens and occasionally handle front desk tasks when overlap happens.

What matters to us: you show up on time, you know your stuff, and you actually care how members are doing. A certification is a starting point — we care more about whether you can coach a real person through a real problem than whether you have the right letters after your name.

This is not a side hustle position. If you are looking for flexible hours around another full-time job, this is probably not the right fit.

To apply: email john@ironstandard.com with a short note about yourself and why this interests you. No formal cover letter needed — just tell us who you are.

Tool 2 — Interview Questions

Generic interview questions get generic answers. This prompt generates questions specific to what actually matters at a small gym — the judgment calls, the member interactions, and the cultural fit that are impossible to see on a resume.

Generate an interview question set for hiring a [ROLE] at my gym.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

What matters most in this specific hire (pick 3–5):
[e.g., reliability, member empathy, technical coaching knowledge, initiative, culture fit, ability to take feedback, handling conflict]

Generate 10 interview questions that:
- Are specific to what matters at a small gym — not generic HR questions
- Mix past-behavior questions ("tell me about a time when..."), situational questions ("how would you handle..."), and values questions
- Include at least 2 questions that reveal whether this person takes initiative or waits to be directed
- Include at least 1 question that reveals how they handle a difficult member interaction
- Are phrased the way a gym owner would actually ask them — conversational, not corporate

After the questions, add a one-sentence scoring guide for each: what does a good answer actually sound like?
Example Output — Coach Interview Questions

1. "Walk me through how you coached someone who was technically inconsistent — not wrong, just inconsistent. What did you do?" · Good answer: specific, shows patience and a system, not just "I told them to fix it."

2. "Tell me about a time a member pushed back on your programming or coaching. What happened?" · Good answer: held their position while staying respectful, or genuinely updated their view based on new information. Neither pure capitulation nor stubbornness.

3. "You notice a member has been coming less frequently. Nobody told you to reach out. What do you do?" · Good answer: reaches out proactively and unprompted. Red flag: waits for the owner to say something.

4. "What does a bad training session feel like to you — as the coach?" · Good answer: shows self-awareness and specific standards, not just "when members don't try hard enough."

5. "Our gym has a specific way we warm up and a specific movement standard for the squat. You disagree with part of it. What do you do?" · Good answer: raises it privately, asks questions, follows the standard until it changes. Red flag: just does it their own way.

Tool 3 — First-Week Onboarding Plan

Most new staff get a tour and a T-shirt. Then they are on their own. This prompt builds a real first-week plan — day by day — with a culture briefing for Day 1 and a list of the five things new hires always get wrong.

Write a first-week onboarding plan for a new [ROLE] at my gym.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

New hire details:
- Name: [NAME]
- Start date: [DATE]
- Previous relevant experience: [BACKGROUND, or "limited — starting fresh"]
- What they need to handle independently within 30 days: [LIST 3–5 things]

Build a day-by-day plan for their first five days that covers:
- Day 1: Everything they need to know before they interact with a single member
- Days 2–3: What to observe and shadow, and what questions to be asking
- Days 4–5: Their first independent responsibilities — what they should own by Friday

Also give me:
- A "culture briefing" — 4–6 sentences I say out loud on their first morning that explains how we operate and what matters here. Not an employee handbook — the things that actually define us.
- A list of 5 things new hires at gyms typically get wrong in their first month, so I can address them before they happen.

Keep it practical. This is not a corporate HR document.
Example Output — Culture Briefing

"Here is what actually matters here. Members are not clients, they are people we know — learn their names in the first two weeks and use them every time. If something is not right and you see it, handle it or tell me; do not wait to see if someone else noticed. Our standard for being on time is that you are ready before the first member walks in, not when the clock says the class starts. If you are not sure about something, ask — I would rather answer the same question three times than have you guess wrong once. And last: the work here is coaching, but the job is relationships. Keep that order and everything else falls into place."

Tool 4 — Offer or Rejection Message

Short prompts for two situations: offering the job (clear, warm, specific) and declining a candidate (respectful, brief, no door slammed).

Write a job offer message for a candidate I want to hire.

[PASTE YOUR GYM PROFILE HERE]

Details:
- Candidate's name: [NAME]
- Role: [TITLE]
- Start date: [DATE]
- Pay: [AMOUNT and structure — hourly / salary / per class]
- Hours: [WEEKLY HOURS OR SCHEDULE]
- Next step to accept: [What they need to do — reply to confirm, sign a document, come in to meet, etc.]

Write a short, warm offer message — under 150 words — that feels like it came from a person, not an HR system. State the terms clearly. End with genuine enthusiasm, not corporate filler.
Write a rejection message for a candidate I interviewed but am not moving forward with.

- Candidate's name: [NAME]
- Role they applied for: [TITLE]
- Reason (do not need to share this — just for tone calibration): [BRIEF REASON]
- Did they interview or just apply: [INTERVIEWED / APPLIED ONLY]

Write a brief, respectful rejection — under 80 words. Thank them genuinely, decline clearly, and leave on good terms. Do not be vague or use phrases like "we will keep your resume on file" unless we actually will.

A note on using these tools

Hiring is one of the highest-leverage things you do as a gym owner. A great coach compounds in value — better member retention, better culture, better results. A wrong hire costs you in time, morale, and money for months.

Claude does not pick the right person for you. It helps you design a process that gives you better signal — a posting that attracts the right applicants, questions that reveal what actually matters, and an onboarding plan that sets the new hire up to succeed instead of sink.

Use these tools as a starting point, then make them yours. The job posting especially should sound like you — read it before you post and adjust anything that feels off.
